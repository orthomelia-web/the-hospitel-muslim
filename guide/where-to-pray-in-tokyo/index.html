Where to pray in Tokyo — airports, sightseeing spots, and everywhere in between

Five prayers a day do not pause for travel. The good news: praying in Tokyo is far easier than it was even five years ago — both airports have proper prayer rooms with wudu, major sights keep dedicated prayer spaces, and Japan is a genuinely tolerant place to pray quietly when no room exists. Here is the practical map.

Checked August 2026 · Rooms and hours change — the official links below are the source of truth

01Landing and departing: airport prayer rooms

Haneda Airport (HND)羽田空港

Wudu facilitiesTerminals 2 & 3

Haneda keeps prayer rooms with qibla markings and ablution areas in Terminal 3 (3rd floor, landside — two rooms) and Terminal 2 (international area: 3rd floor landside, plus rooms airside on the 2nd floor). Terminal 1 has no prayer room — if you fly a domestic leg from T1, pray at T2 first; the free inter-terminal shuttle takes minutes. The Haneda Airport Garden complex, connected to T3, also has a fully equipped prayer room.

Narita Airport (NRT)成田空港

Wudu facilitiesBoth terminals

Narita has prayer rooms in both Terminal 1 and Terminal 2, and — usefully — on both sides of passport control, so you can pray after landing or after security without racing the clock. Rooms are signposted in English; staff at any information counter will point the way.

02Praying while sightseeing

Tokyo's big attractions have quietly become good at this. Three worth knowing, all on the Muslim traveler's usual route:

  • Tokyo Skytree Town (Solamachi) — the tower complex 15 minutes from our villa keeps an official prayer space with men's and women's areas and prayer mats. Locations have moved between floors over the years, so ask at any information counter; they are used to the question.
  • Laox Akihabara main store — the duty-free electronics store keeps a prayer room on the 7th floor. Handy mid-shopping, minutes from Akihabara Station.
  • Masjid As-Salaam (Okachimachi) — a real mosque squeezed between Ueno and Akihabara, perfect when your day covers Ueno Park, Ameyoko market or the electronics district.

Department stores increasingly keep a prayer or multi-faith room too — when in doubt, ask the information counter on the ground floor: "Is there a prayer room?" is understood everywhere tourists go.

03The mosques worth planning a day around

Tokyo Camii (Yoyogi-Uehara) is Japan's largest mosque and its most beautiful building for jumu'ah; Masjid Darul Arqam in Asakusa is our neighborhood mosque, a 20–25 minute walk from the villa, with a warm international congregation. Both — plus halal groceries and restaurants around them — are covered street by street in our halal food & mosques guide.

04When there is no prayer room

Some days the schedule wins. Know this: praying in public in Japan is entirely legal and almost always met with polite indifference or quiet curiosity — never hostility. A few habits make it graceful:

  • Carry a pocket prayer mat. A folded travel mat weighs nothing and turns any clean, quiet corner — a park, a station's calm end, a temple garden's edge — into a musalla.
  • Use a qibla app (Muslim Pro, Qibla Finder in Google) — Tokyo's qibla points roughly west-northwest.
  • Pick the quiet spot, not the hidden one. A bench area or park corner reads as natural; ducking behind machinery draws more attention than praying openly and calmly.
  • Combine when your fiqh allows it. Travelers shortening and joining prayers will find Tokyo's distances a legitimate reason more often than not.

05And at home base: two prayer rooms of your own

This is the quiet luxury of a whole-house stay. At THE HOSPITEL, two dedicated prayer spaces with qibla markings wait at the end of every day — no furniture to rearrange, no roommates to time around. Prayer mats, a Quran and hijabs are already there. Fajr in congregation with your family, in your own house, is the way Tokyo mornings should begin.
